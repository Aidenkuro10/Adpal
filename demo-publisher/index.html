Essay

A grey rectangle, and what it cost us

The first banner ad went live in October 1994. It was a grey rectangle the size of a business card, it asked a disarmingly simple question about your desktop, and nearly half the people who saw it clicked. That number has never been repeated — for the entirely predictable reason that the web spent the next thirty years making the deal worse for everyone except the people selling it.

The modern page is a small miracle of extraction. Before the first paragraph arrives, a dozen companies have already bid on the right to interrupt it. Your scroll position, your device, your approximate location and the fact that you paused on a paragraph about mortgages are all packaged, priced and sold in the time it takes a browser to render a typeface. The publisher receives a fraction of a cent. You receive a slightly slower page.

The worst deal in media

Somewhere between the pop-up and the pre-roll, the transaction changed shape. You stopped being the audience and became the inventory. The product was never the newspaper, the feed or the video: it was the reliable, measurable fact that you would look at something for a moment before scrolling away.

It is worth saying plainly what that arrangement asks of you. It asks for your time, your attention and a surprisingly detailed record of your behaviour — and it returns, at best, an article you did not pay for. The bargain is not dishonest. It is simply unexamined, in the way that all very old bargains become.

What if the ad paid you?

A few publishers have started testing the opposite arrangement. It begins with an uncomfortable premise: attention is labour, and labour should be paid. Not metaphorically, not in “exposure”, not in a coupon for a service you did not ask for. Paid, in the same way a freelancer is paid for an afternoon of work, into an account with a balance and a statement.

The mechanism is deliberately boring, which is the highest compliment one can pay a payment system. A publisher declares a placement. The reader scrolls past it. Something measures whether a human being was actually there — not a click, not a hover, not a timer that runs while the tab sits in the background — and if the answer is yes, an invoice is issued and split before the week is out.

Two things follow. The first is that the reader stops being inventory and becomes a counterparty, with a price and a receipt. The second is subtler: a page paid per verified look is a page paid to be readable, because the only way to earn the next impression is to deserve the current one.

The invoice for your attention

A single impression is worth two cents. That is not a life-changing sum, and nobody involved pretends otherwise. What matters is that it is legible: it has a unit, a recipient, a split and a timestamp. You can look at it and say what was bought and who was paid.

Legibility changes behaviour on both sides of the transaction. The advertiser can no longer buy a thousand impressions and hope; it buys attention that was measured. The publisher cannot bury the placement behind three interstitials; the placement has to be seen to be paid. And the reader, for the first time, has a line item with their name on it — small, but not imaginary.

The awkward part

Here is the objection that any honest version of this essay has to confront: it is still advertising. A paid impression is still an interruption. Handing the reader a receipt does not make the banner disappear, and a market for attention does not automatically produce better journalism — it produces more of whatever gets looked at.

The counter-argument is not that advertising has become virtuous. It is that the current arrangement hides its costs, and a hidden cost is a cost that can grow without limit. A placement that names its price, measures its audience with consent and pays the person doing the looking is at least a transaction you can refuse. That is a lower bar than “fixed”, and a much higher one than what we have now.

The first banner asked whether you had ever clicked on a banner. The answer, for most of us, is no. The more interesting question is what happens when the banner has to prove it is being seen before it can appear at all.

The takeaway

The internet did not become hostile by accident. It became hostile because attention was free to take and impossible to invoice. Putting a price on the look — and paying the person who gave it — is a modest fix for a very old problem: the banner is not going away, but it might, at last, start paying rent.
